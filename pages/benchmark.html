Image compression benchmark: JPEG vs WebP vs AVIF vs PNG

How much smaller does each format actually make an image, and what does it cost in quality? We ran the encoders this site uses on a fixed set of public-domain images and measured both.

By the Compressly team · Published and updated 24 September 2026

Key findings

At the same visual quality, AVIF photos were a median 19% smaller than MozJPEG and WebP photos 13% smaller — well below the 25–50% figures usually quoted, largely because those compare against a standard JPEG encoder rather than an optimised one. On screenshots and flat graphics the gap is far wider.

  • Photos, matched quality: AVIF was 17–29% smaller than a quality-75 MozJPEG of equal SSIM; WebP was 5–20% smaller.
  • Screenshot and flat graphic, matched quality: WebP was 55–59% smaller than JPEG and AVIF 59–81% smaller. Sharp edges and flat colour are where JPEG is weakest.
  • The same quality number is not the same quality. At quality 75, AVIF files were larger than WebP on all four photos — but they also scored higher SSIM (median 0.994 against 0.985 across all seven images). Comparing formats at equal slider values says little.
  • Lossless PNG optimisation is worth doing: OxiPNG cut unoptimised PNGs by a median 51% (range 31–74%) without changing a pixel, and lossless WebP came out a further 16–55% smaller than the OxiPNG file (median 24%).
  • Any lossy format at quality 75 removed 88–97% of an unoptimised PNG photo's size. If your photographs are stored as PNG, the format change matters far more than which modern format you pick.

Why these numbers are lower than you may have read. Google's WebP compression study reported WebP 25–34% smaller than JPEG at equal SSIM, measured against a standard JPEG encoder. MozJPEG is itself an improved JPEG encoder that produces smaller files at the same quality, so part of that headroom is already gone before WebP or AVIF are involved. Against an optimised JPEG, the honest photo figure is closer to 10–20%.

The test images

Seven images chosen to cover the cases people actually compress, all public domain or our own work:

  • Four photographs from NASA's image library: an Apollo 11 astronaut on the Moon (AS11-40-5903), the Apollo 17 whole-Earth photograph (AS17-148-22727), flowers across Kennedy Space Center dunes (KSC-03PD-2389, the hardest image here because of its dense detail), and an owl nest (KSC-2014-1791). Each was resized to 1,600 px on the long side.
  • A UI screenshot, 1440×900, of this site's PNG to AVIF page — text, flat panels and one accent colour.
  • A transparent product cutout, a coffee cup rendered from a CC0 SVG at 1,200 px, standing in for the product shots marketplaces ask for.
  • A flat graphic, the flag of Brazil rendered from its public-domain SVG at 1,400 px: hard edges and a handful of colours.

Method

  • Encoders: the jSquash WebAssembly builds of MozJPEG, libwebp, libavif and OxiPNG that Compressly runs in your browser, at the settings in the app's defaults. Exact versions are recorded in the raw data.
  • Baseline: each image written as a plain PNG with no optimisation — roughly what an image editor or screenshot tool saves.
  • Quality metric: SSIM (structural similarity) on luminance, after compositing any transparency over white, using 8×8 windows at a stride of 4. Every output is decoded again and compared with the source.
  • Matched quality: a binary search over each encoder's quality setting for the smallest file whose SSIM is at least the JPEG's.
  • Integrity: each source image is checked against a SHA-256 hash of its decoded pixels before it is measured, so every run uses identical inputs.

The raw results, including codec versions and per-image scores, are available as benchmark.json.

Limitations

  • Seven images is a small sample. It shows the shape of the trade-offs, not a universal percentage — your own images will vary.
  • SSIM on luminance does not see colour errors, and no single metric captures what a person notices. AVIF's colour handling is not credited here.
  • The photographs were JPEGs when NASA published them. Downscaling to 1,600 px removes most of the original compression artefacts, but not every trace.
  • Measurements were taken with the single-threaded builds under Node.js. The browser's multi-threaded AVIF encoder is the same libavif release, but threading can change the output slightly.
  • Encoding time is not reported: it depends on the machine, and a number from ours would not predict yours.
